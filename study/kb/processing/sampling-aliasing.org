#+title: Sampling theorem and aliasing
#+summary: A signal sampled every \(\Delta\) represents frequencies up to the Nyquist limit \(1/(2\Delta)\); finer detail is not lost but folded back as false low-frequency patterns (aliasing, moiré); sensors limit it with the blur of their PSF, and image resampling must low-pass before decimating.

* Statement
If the spectrum of \(c(x)\) is zero for \(|f|>B\), sampling with \(\Delta\le1/(2B)\) allows exact reconstruction (sinc interpolation). Otherwise a component at frequency \(f\) appears at the alias \(f-k/\Delta\) inside \([-1/(2\Delta),1/(2\Delta)]\).

* Derivation
Sampling multiplies \(c(x)\) by a comb of period \(\Delta\); in frequency this convolves the spectrum with a comb of period \(1/\Delta\) ([[id:convolution-theorem][convolution theorem]]): copies of the spectrum repeat every \(1/\Delta\). They do not overlap only if the bandwidth \(2B\le1/\Delta\).

Sampled once per pixel, a pattern of 0.9 cycles per pixel is indistinguishable from one of 0.1.

* Example
Rows of vines 2.5 m apart imaged with 10 m pixels are far beyond the Nyquist limit (20 m period): they can appear as broad fake stripes. Downsampling a 10 m image to 30 m by taking every third pixel aliases detail; averaging \(3\times3\) blocks first (a low-pass) avoids it.

* Intuition
A wheel filmed at too low a frame rate seems to turn slowly backwards: the samples cannot tell a fast turn from a slow one.

* Pitfall
Aliasing cannot be removed after sampling: once folded, true and false frequencies are mixed. It must be prevented before (optics, detector size, anti-alias filtering).
