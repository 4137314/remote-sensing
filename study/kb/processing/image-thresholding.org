#+title: Histogram thresholding
#+summary: When the histogram is bimodal, a threshold \(T\) between the modes splits the image into two classes; Otsu's method chooses \(T\) maximising the between-class variance \(\omega_0\omega_1(\mu_0-\mu_1)^2\), and with Gaussian models the Bayes threshold is where the weighted class densities cross.

* Method
For a threshold \(T\): class 0 = \(\{\mathrm{DN}\le T\}\), class 1 = \(\{\mathrm{DN}>T\}\), with weights \(\omega_0(T)=H(T)\), \(\omega_1=1-\omega_0\), means \(\mu_0,\mu_1\). Otsu:
\[ T^\ast=\arg\max_T\ \sigma_B^2(T)=\omega_0\,\omega_1\,(\mu_0-\mu_1)^2, \]
equivalent to minimising the within-class [[id:image-statistics][variance]]; computed in one pass over the histogram. Bayesian alternative: fit two densities and put \(T\) where \(P_0p_0(T)=P_1p_1(T)\) ([[id:bayes-classifier][minimum error]]).

* Example
Water mapping: water is dark in NIR and in SAR intensity, land bright; one threshold gives a binary water mask.
#+begin_src python
  from skimage.filters import threshold_otsu
  T = threshold_otsu(nir); water = nir < T
#+end_src

* Intuition
Look at the valley between the two hills of the histogram and cut there.

* Pitfall
A threshold uses no spatial information: noise and mixed pixels produce isolated errors; and if one class is tiny, its hill is invisible and Otsu splits the dominant class instead.
