#+title: Spectral signature
#+summary: The spectral signature of a surface is its reflectance as a function of wavelength, \(\rho(\lambda)\); different materials have different shapes — vegetation low in the red and high in the NIR, water dark beyond the visible, soil rising slowly — but each class is a random family of curves, so discrimination is statistical.

* Definition
\(\rho(\lambda)=E_r(\lambda)/E_i(\lambda)\) as a function of \(\lambda\) depends on the composition, structure and geometry of the surface. Typical shapes:
- *green vegetation*: chlorophyll absorbs blue and red, small green peak (~0.55 µm), steep rise (red edge, ~0.7 µm) to a high NIR plateau due to leaf cell structure, dips at the water absorption bands 1.4 and 1.9 µm;
- *water*: low in the visible, almost zero in NIR and SWIR;
- *bare soil*: increases slowly with \(\lambda\), lower with moisture and organic matter;
- *snow*: very high in the visible, dropping in the SWIR (snow and cloud separable there).

* Variability
A given land cover shows many realisations: static factors (slope, exposure) and dynamic ones (soil moisture, growth stage, atmospheric transmittance) change the curve. The signature of a class is therefore a random process, and recognition must model its statistics ([[id:feature-space][feature space]], [[id:bayes-classifier][Bayes]]).

* Intuition
Materials have colours beyond the visible; the infrared "colour" of vegetation is its brightest feature.

* Pitfall
A multispectral sensor samples the signature in a few broad bands: two materials with different fine signatures can give identical band values. Hyperspectral sensors reduce this ambiguity.
