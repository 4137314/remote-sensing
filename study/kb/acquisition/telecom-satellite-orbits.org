#+title: Orbits of telecommunication satellites
#+summary: Telecommunication satellites use geostationary orbits (one satellite covers 43% of the Earth), highly elliptical 64° orbits with 12 h period (three satellites cover high latitudes), MEO at about 10 000 km and LEO constellations at about 1000 km (dozens of satellites, short delays); the choice depends on area, latitude, elevation angle, delay and launcher.

* Definition
| orbit | inclination | altitude | period | example |
|---+---+---+---+---|
| highly elliptical | ~64° | apogee ~40 000 km, perigee ~550 km | ~12 h | Molniya: 3 satellites for global high-latitude coverage |
| LEO | ~90° | ~1000 km | ~1 h 30 min | Iridium, Globalstar constellations |
| MEO | ~50° | ~10 000 km | ~6 h | a dozen satellites, e.g. Inmarsat-type services |
| GEO | 0° | 35 786 km | 24 h | broadcast, fixed services |
The 64° inclination cancels the rotation of the line of apsides, keeping the apogee over the same hemisphere, where the satellite lingers.

Why satellites for telecommunications: wide coverage from one position, natural broadcasting, large bandwidth, reconfigurability.

* Criteria
- extent and latitude of the area to cover;
- elevation angle: mobile and GPS users need satellites high in the sky to avoid trees and buildings;
- propagation delay and visibility duration;
- interference with other satellites;
- launcher capacity.

* Intuition
GEO is one big fixed antenna in the sky; LEO is a swarm of small fast ones handing users over to each other.

* Pitfall
LEO constellations need many satellites and inter-satellite links or many ground stations, since each satellite is visible from a point for only a few minutes.
