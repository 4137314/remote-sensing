#+title: Multispectral and hyperspectral images
#+summary: A multispectral scanner measures a few broad bands, a hyperspectral one hundreds of narrow contiguous bands; either way the image is a cube \(I(x,y,b)\), and each pixel is a vector of \(B\) values that samples the spectral signature of its ground cell.

* Model
\[ I(x,y,b)=\int\!\!\int\!\!\int_{\text{cell}(x,y)\times\text{band }b} L(x',y',\lambda)\,dx'\,dy'\,d\lambda, \]
for \(b=1,\dots,B\).
- *Multispectral*: \(B\approx4\)–15, bands of 30–200 nm placed in atmospheric windows and on key features (red, NIR, SWIR). Example: Landsat TM, Sentinel-2 (13 bands).
- *Hyperspectral* (imaging spectrometers, since the 1980s): \(B\approx100\)–400 contiguous bands of ~10 nm over 0.4–2.5 µm (AVIRIS, PRISMA). Fine sampling of the signature allows identifying materials (minerals, pigments) and estimating physical parameters, at the cost of huge data volumes, redundancy between bands and lower SNR per band.

Two spatial axes and one spectral axis; a pixel is a line through the cube.

* Intuition
A colour photo has three bands; a hyperspectral image is a photo with a spectrometer behind every pixel.

* Pitfall
More bands do not automatically give better classification: with few training samples, high dimensionality makes estimates unreliable (Hughes phenomenon), so feature selection or extraction is needed.
