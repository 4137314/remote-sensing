#+title: Satellite orbits
#+summary: An orbit is an ellipse (or circle) with the Earth at a focus, described by its inclination, its ascending and descending nodes, apogee and perigee; Kepler's third law \(T=2\pi\sqrt{a^3/\mu}\) ties the period to the semi-major axis, so altitude fixes the period.

* Definition
- *Inclination* \(i\): angle between the orbital plane and the equator (\(i=0^\circ\) equatorial, \(\approx90^\circ\) polar).
- *Ascending / descending node*: where the satellite crosses the equator going north / south.
- *Apogee / perigee*: farthest / nearest points; the satellite is fastest at perigee, slowest at apogee (Kepler's second law).
- *Period*: for semi-major axis \(a\) and \(\mu=GM_\oplus=3.986\times10^{14}\) m\(^3\)s\(^{-2}\),
\[ T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{\mu}}. \]

* Derivation
For a circular orbit of radius \(r\), gravity provides the centripetal force: \(\mu/r^2=\omega^2r\), so \(\omega=\sqrt{\mu/r^3}\) and \(T=2\pi/\omega\). At 700 km altitude, \(r=7078\) km: \(T\approx5930\) s \(\approx99\) min, about 14.5 orbits per day.

* Intuition
Altitude is the master parameter: it sets the period, the speed over the ground, the footprint and the resolution.

* Pitfall
Orbits are not fixed: the Earth's oblateness (\(J_2\)) makes the orbital plane precess, and drag lowers LEO orbits. [[id:sun-synchronous-orbit][Sun-synchronous]] orbits exploit the first; station-keeping manoeuvres fight the second.
