#+title: Chirp and pulse compression
#+summary: Instead of a very short, very powerful pulse, radars transmit a long linear-frequency-modulated pulse (chirp) of bandwidth \(B\) and correlate the echo with a replica (matched filter); the output peak has width \(\approx1/B\), so the range resolution becomes \(c/(2B)\), independent of the pulse duration.

* Construction
Chirp of duration \(\tau\) and rate \(k\): \(s(t)=\mathrm{rect}(t/\tau)\cos\!\big(2\pi f_0t+\pi k t^2\big)\), instantaneous frequency \(f_0+kt\), bandwidth \(B=k\tau\). The matched filter (correlation with \(s\)) compresses the echo into a sinc-like peak of width \(1/B\):
\[ r_{SR}=\frac{c}{2B},\qquad r_{GR}=\frac{c}{2B\sin\theta}. \]
The compression ratio \(\tau B\) (time–bandwidth product, typically 100–1000) is the gain in peak-to-energy ratio.

A chirp: frequency grows linearly during the pulse.

* Example
[[id:copernicus-sentinel][Sentinel-1]] uses chirps of up to 100 MHz bandwidth: \(c/(2B)=1.5\) m in [[id:side-looking-radar][slant range]], a few metres on the ground, with pulses tens of microseconds long.

* Intuition
A long whistle that rises in pitch: each instant of the echo is labelled by its frequency, so the receiver can tell overlapping echoes apart and re-stack them into a sharp click.

* Pitfall
Compression produces sidelobes around strong targets; they are reduced by weighting (windowing) the filter, at the cost of a slightly wider peak.
